# Wealth & Investing

How DINCR reasons about when capital is investable, risk, horizon, diversification, allocation and wealth optimization — and where the product's boundaries lie. Uncertain returns are never presented as guaranteed, and generative AI does not produce specific production investment recommendations.

## 1. When capital is actually investable

Capital is investable only if **all** hold:
- essentials and contractual obligations are covered (not in survival/stability repair);
- starter buffer and contextual full reserve are at target (or a deliberate, explained exception);
- no high-cost debt remains (or the user knowingly accepts the tradeoff, documented);
- known short-term spending and short-horizon goals are funded separately;
- the money will not be needed within the investment horizon;
- the user can tolerate a meaningful temporary loss without being forced to sell.

If any condition is unknown, the investable amount is **undetermined**, not assumed.

Exceptions that may legitimately run earlier: mandatory pension contributions, employer/association matches or mandatory savings schemes. These follow their own rules, not DINCR discretionary allocation.

## 2. Liquidity prerequisites

Investing locks money into instruments that may fall in value or charge exit penalties exactly when a shock hits. The reserve exists so that investments are never the emergency fund. A portfolio that must be sold during a downturn to pay rent converts a temporary paper loss into a permanent one.

## 3. Risk capacity vs risk preference

- **Risk capacity (objective):** how much loss the user's finances can absorb without harming essentials or goals. Driven by reserve, income stability, horizon, obligations, dependents, total wealth.
- **Risk preference/tolerance (subjective):** how much volatility the user is comfortable with.

The appropriate risk level is bounded by the **lower** of the two. A risk-loving user with low capacity should not take high risk; a risk-averse user with high capacity may reasonably stay conservative (with the opportunity cost explained, not overridden).

Preference is self-reported and can change after losses; capacity is computed and can change after life events.

## 4. Time horizon

- < 1 year: not an investment horizon; hold liquid, stable instruments.
- 1–5 years: limited volatility tolerance; the goal date matters.
- > 5 years: volatility can be tolerated if capacity allows; sequence risk still matters near the goal date.

Horizon is per-goal, not per-user. The same user can have a 6-month house-deposit goal and a 25-year retirement goal.

## 5. Diversification and concentration risk

- Diversification across asset classes, issuers, sectors, geographies and currencies reduces the chance that one event destroys wealth.
- **Concentration risks** common in personal finance:
  - most wealth in one property;
  - most wealth in one's own business or employer's stock (income and wealth fail together);
  - single-currency exposure when obligations are in another currency;
  - single-institution exposure (deposit guarantees have limits; verify current rules);
  - illiquid solidarity-association or pension balances dominating net worth.
- DINCR can compute concentration indicators (share of net worth in the largest asset/category) and explain them. It should not prescribe specific products.

## 6. Debt vs investing

From the investing side:
- Paying debt = certain return equal to effective cost.
- Investing = uncertain return; expected value is an estimate with a range.
- Compare certain cost against a **conservative** expected return range, adjusted for risk, fees and taxes. When the ranges overlap, both paths are defensible; explain the tradeoff and respect preference within capacity.
- Never frame investing while carrying high-cost debt as "making money" without showing the guaranteed cost of the debt.

## 7. Opportunity cost

Every allocation forgoes another. DINCR explanations should show the main alternative forgone:
- cash above reserve target → forgone expected returns, inflation erosion;
- investing → forgone guaranteed debt savings or liquidity;
- prepaying low-cost debt → forgone liquidity and diversification.

Opportunity cost is an estimate, stated as a range with assumptions.

## 8. Expected-return uncertainty

- Historical returns are not guarantees; ranges and possibilities of loss must be shown with any projection.
- Projections must state assumptions (return, inflation, fees, contributions, horizon) and show at least a conservative scenario.
- Avoid single-point forecasts presented as outcomes ("you will have ₡X").
- Nominal vs real (inflation-adjusted) values must be labeled.
- Currency movements add uncertainty for foreign-currency investments.

## 9. Long-term compounding

- Compounding rewards time and consistency more than timing.
- Fees and taxes compound too; small recurring fees materially reduce long-horizon outcomes.
- Regular contributions (from surplus) matter more than return optimization for low- and mid-capital users.
- Compounding illustrations are educational; label them as hypothetical.

## 10. Allocation concepts

Concepts DINCR may explain and use in deterministic, approved rules:
- **Strategic allocation:** the long-run mix across risk levels derived from capacity, preference and horizon.
- **Goal-based allocation:** each goal gets a risk level matching its horizon.
- **Rebalancing:** returning to target mix periodically or when drift exceeds a band.
- **Glide path:** reducing risk as a goal date approaches.
- **Liquidity ladder:** staggering maturities of fixed-term instruments so some money becomes available regularly.

Any concrete allocation percentages used in production must come from a versioned, human-approved rule and comply with local regulation — not from AI generation at runtime.

## 11. Low-capital users

- The priority is building resilience and a contribution habit; returns on small balances matter less than consistency.
- Avoid products whose fees or minimums consume small balances.
- Don't encourage speculative or high-risk activity to "catch up".
- Small, automatic, surplus-based contributions after reserve are the main lever.

## 12. High-capital users

- High capital does not mean investable capital; decompose by liquidity and restriction first.
- Risks shift toward concentration, liquidity mismatch, idle cash beyond reserve, fees, taxes, estate/succession and protection.
- Large idle cash beyond reserve and known needs is an optimization opportunity (and inflation loss), but moving it is a decision the user makes, ideally with a licensed adviser.
- A high-capital user can still have a liquidity problem (property-rich, cash-poor) — resolve that before optimization.

## 13. Wealth optimization

For users whose lower levels are resolved:
- review concentration and diversification;
- cash drag (excess cash beyond needs);
- fee drag;
- tax efficiency (jurisdiction-specific; refer to professionals);
- alignment of each goal's risk with its horizon;
- protection (insurance adequacy) and estate planning concepts;
- income diversification.

Optimization is incremental and never at the cost of re-creating a liquidity or resilience gap.

## 14. Product boundaries (regulated advice)

- DINCR provides financial organization, education and deterministic planning tools. It does not act as a licensed investment adviser, broker, tax adviser or lawyer.
- Securities advice and brokerage in Costa Rica are regulated (e.g., by the securities supervisor); verify current regulatory requirements before any feature that could be construed as personalized investment advice.
- No generative-AI-produced specific security, fund or product recommendations in production.
- Educational content must be clearly labeled as general and not individualized advice.
- Suggest consulting a licensed professional for large or complex decisions (windfalls, inheritances, business sales, tax planning).
- Never guarantee returns, never imply certainty about markets, never promote speculative instruments.

## 15. Edge cases

- All reserve targets met but high-cost debt remains → investable = 0 by default (or explicit, documented exception).
- Investable amount computed but income highly variable → larger reserve before investing.
- Large goal due in 18 months → money excluded from long-term investable pool.
- User's net worth concentrated in employer equity → concentration flag.
- User holds investments and is behind on a critical debt → survival override; explain sale tradeoffs without instructing specific trades.
- Negative real return on excess cash → opportunity cost shown as an estimate.
- User reports high risk tolerance but low capacity → risk bounded by capacity; explain.
- Missing asset valuations → net worth "incomplete", not fabricated.
- Foreign-currency holdings → valued with dated rates; conversion labeled.
